GA 400 Express Lanes: Tolls, Timeline and the Debate

By Alpharetta List staffPublished November 13, 20266 min read

Construction on the GA 400 express lanes started in April 2026, and for Alpharetta commuters the next five years will mean barrier walls, lane shifts and, eventually, a choice every morning: pay a toll or sit in the free lanes. The project promises faster trips and a path for transit, but the toll model, the price tag and the construction headaches have people arguing. Here are the facts and the main arguments on each side.

The facts

  • What: 16 miles of new express (toll) lanes plus a dedicated busway along GA 400, from the North Springs MARTA station to about a mile north of McFarland Parkway in Forsyth County. More than 6.5 miles run through Alpharetta.
  • When: construction officially began April 22, 2026, and is expected to take about five years, with completion in early 2031.
  • Who: a private company, SR 400 Peach Partners, will run the lanes. In exchange for 50 years of toll revenue, it funds construction and maintenance for the length of the contract. That is different from the publicly run express lanes on I-75 and I-85.
  • How tolls work: prices change with real-time traffic. When speeds drop below 45 mph, the operator can raise rates, and the toll cap adjusts with Georgia economic data for growth and inflation.
  • The deal: the AJC has reported the public-private partnership is valued at $11 billion, including a $3.8 billion concession payment from the private company.

What changes for Alpharetta drivers now

The northbound GA 400 flex lane between just south of the Webb Bridge Road overpass and exit 11 (Windward Parkway) closes permanently starting December 13, 2026, so crews can build the express lanes. Four northbound lanes stay open, and drivers headed to Windward Parkway should use the right lanes. Expect temporary concrete barriers, shifting lanes and work zones up and down the corridor; GDOT advises checking 511 or 511ga.org before you go.

The case for

A reliable option when you need it. Supporters say express lanes give drivers a guaranteed faster trip when it matters most, like a daycare pickup or a flight. The State Road and Tollway Authority's Ericka Bayonne described it as paying for time savings: express lane users are choosing to get to work on time.

Taxpayers do not foot the whole bill. GDOT's Tim Matthews argues that public-private partnerships let the state build more than it could afford alone. "We get more than we can afford when we go that way," he said.

Transit and carpools ride free. The lanes and busway are designed to carry buses and registered carpool vans, and officials point to more than 1.7 million free bus and carpool trips on metro express lanes in 2025. Supporters see GA 400 as the backbone for future bus rapid transit to north Fulton.

Everyone benefits. Matthews argues that moving some drivers into express lanes helps people who stay in the free lanes too.

The case against

Affordability. Critics worry about a two-tier highway where those who can pay move and those who cannot sit. WSB-TV spoke with a North Carolina driver whose monthly I-77 express lane costs rose from about $30 to about $160 within a few years, and reported that tolls there came in at double the original projections.

Fifty years is a long time. Handing toll revenue to a private operator for half a century means future residents will live with decisions made today. Officials say the contract includes guardrails on affordability and access; skeptics want to see how those guardrails work in practice.

Will congestion really drop? The AJC noted that data suggests congestion may climb back up after the project is complete, and WSB reported that GDOT did not provide data showing existing express lanes reduced congestion in the free lanes.

Five years of construction. Commuters face lane closures, the loss of the northbound flex lane near Windward and tree clearing along the corridor before any benefit arrives.

Alpharetta's past frustration. Back in 2019, Alpharetta asked GDOT for a signature interchange at Encore Parkway instead of the planned access near Webb Bridge Road. GDOT declined, saying the request did not fit the project timeline, and moved ahead with access between Webb Bridge and Windward Parkway.

Tips for the construction years

  • Check 511ga.org or the Georgia 511 app before you leave.
  • Leave extra time around exits 7 through 12, where work is active.
  • Consider the Alpha Loop or a bike for short trips between downtown and Avalon.
  • Remote or flexible schedules can help skip the worst peak hours.

Frequently asked questions

When will the GA 400 express lanes open?

Construction began April 22, 2026 and is expected to take about five years, with completion expected in early 2031.

Is the GA 400 flex lane closing?

Yes. The northbound flex lane from just south of the Webb Bridge Road overpass to exit 11 (Windward Parkway) closes permanently starting December 13, 2026 for express lane construction. Four northbound lanes stay open.

How much will the GA 400 tolls cost?

Tolls will be dynamic, changing with real-time traffic. When speeds drop below 45 mph, the operator can raise rates, and the toll cap adjusts with Georgia economic data for growth and inflation. Exact prices have not been set.

Who will run the GA 400 express lanes?

A private company, SR 400 Peach Partners, will run the lanes. In exchange for 50 years of toll revenue, it funds construction and maintenance for the length of the contract.
