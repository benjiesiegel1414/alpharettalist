How Fast Should Alpharetta Grow? The Density Debate, Explained

By Alpharetta List staffPublished November 13, 20266 min read

"Will we still be Alpharetta?" That question, asked by Council member Katie Reeves during the North Point Mall vote, sums up the biggest argument in town right now. Alpharetta is a city of about 67,000 people that has become one of metro Atlanta's top places to live and work, and every new apartment building, mixed-use project and tree removal now draws a crowd. Here is where the growth debate stands in 2026, what the city's own plans say, and the strongest arguments on each side.

Why this is coming up now

Three things have pushed growth to the top of the agenda this year:

  • The Horizon 2045 Comprehensive Plan. In May 2026 the City Council unanimously sent a draft of its long-range plan to the Atlanta Regional Commission. The plan calls for redevelopment along highway corridors, retrofitting older suburban offices and supporting mixed-use centers, along with goals to reduce traffic, encourage walking and protect trees and historic resources.
  • North Point Mall. The council approved a redevelopment with a 20,000-seat arena and about 1,385 apartments in a 4-3 vote on August 25, 2026. See the North Point arena debate.
  • A steady stream of rezoning fights, from downtown infill to large-lot neighborhoods.

What the numbers say

  • Homeowners vs. renters: Alpharetta's comprehensive plan sets a goal of about 68% homeowners and 32% renters. A city-commissioned housing study by KB Advisory Group found the city is currently at that target.
  • Who is renting: the same study found that over the past 10 years, renters earning more than $100,000 grew by 9%, while renters earning under $50,000 grew by 4%. More professionals are renting as home prices climb.
  • Home prices: new homes are generally priced above $500,000 to $600,000, which the study said puts them out of reach for many entry-level workers.
  • Traffic: about 46,000 vehicles a day pass one stretch of Old Milton Parkway near downtown, according to Georgia DOT data cited in local coverage.

The case for more growth

It is what made Alpharetta. Mayor Jim Gilvin has argued that the balance between growth and quality of life is what turned Alpharetta into an economic hub, and that mixed-use centers help attract employers and support small businesses. He has also said that most of what is great in Alpharetta was built by the private sector, not the city.

Housing costs. With new homes above half a million dollars, supporters of more housing say younger professionals, teachers, first responders and grown children of longtime residents are being priced out. More homes, including townhomes and condos, give them a path in.

Aging office parks and malls. Corridors like North Point and older office campuses are losing value. Supporters say redevelopment keeps the tax base strong without raising residents' property taxes.

Walkability. Projects near downtown and along the Alpha Loop can let more people walk to shops and restaurants instead of driving, which is one of the Horizon 2045 plan's goals.

The case for slowing down

Traffic and services. Many residents say roads, schools and city services are already stretched, and that each new project adds to the strain. Reeves said residents she met while campaigning worry the city is growing too fast and "don't want to be the next Buckhead."

Community character. Critics worry that four-story buildings and dense apartment complexes erode the small-town, family feel that drew them here, especially downtown.

Trees. Tree loss comes up at almost every hearing. In one downtown case, residents asked the council to save a water oak specimen tree; lifelong resident Taylor Melesh said the city has lost too many trees to development. The council approved removal after a city arborist recommended it, citing root damage and the need for wider, safer sidewalks.

Renters and stability. Gilvin has said that keeping a strong share of homeowners helps preserve community, arguing that renters, especially in apartments, move more often and may be less invested in local issues. Others push back that renters are neighbors too and that the label says little about how engaged someone is.

Recent decisions

  • 1425 Mayfield Road (denied, June 22, 2026): the council denied rezoning a 4.6-acre agricultural tract for five homes priced near $4 million each. Neighbors raised traffic, at least 19 specimen trees, stormwater and privacy; more than 20 people signed in support. Council members gave no public explanation for the denial.
  • North Point Mall (approved 4-3, Aug. 25, 2026): a 100-acre arena district with more than 1,300 apartments.
  • Downtown infill: a proposal called Alpharetta District Places, on a block near Old Milton Parkway, Marietta Street and South Main Street, has drawn concerns about traffic on small streets, late-night noise and displacement of existing small businesses, while the developer points to walkability and links to the Alpha Loop.

Where people agree

Even on opposite sides, most residents seem to share a few goals: keep good schools, protect tree canopy, avoid gridlock, keep downtown charming and give the next generation a way to afford a home here. The fight is mostly over how to balance them. As Reeves put it about the comprehensive plan, "It's up to the city to put the guard rails on."

How to weigh in

  • Read the Horizon 2045 plan and upcoming agendas on the city's CivicClerk portal.
  • Attend Planning Commission and City Council meetings at City Hall, where public hearings are held on rezonings.
  • Email the mayor and council through the City of Alpharetta website.
